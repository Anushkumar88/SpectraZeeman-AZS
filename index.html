Physical Comparison: Normal vs. Anomalous Zeeman Effect

Theoretical Foundations

The Zeeman Effect refers to the splitting of atomic spectral lines in an external magnetic field. In the Normal Zeeman Effect, atomic states have zero electron spin ($S = 0$), so $g_1 = g_2 = 1.0$, producing exactly three equidistant lines separated by the Lorentz unit $\Delta\tilde{\nu}_L = \frac{eB}{4\pi m_e c} \approx 0.46686 \times B\text{ cm}^{-1}$.

In the Anomalous Zeeman Effect, non-zero electron spin ($S \neq 0$) couples with orbital angular momentum via spin-orbit coupling. Because the spin gyromagnetic ratio ($g_S \approx 2$) is double that of orbital motion ($g_L = 1$), the total magnetic moment is not parallel to total angular momentum. The effective Landé $g$-factor is:

g_J = 1 + [J(J + 1) + S(S + 1) - L(L + 1)] / [2 · J · (J + 1)]

This lifts the degeneracy of the magnetic sub-levels, yielding complex multiplets of 4, 6, 9, or more lines with unequal separations.

🔬 Laboratory Apparatus Description

In a physical physics laboratory, the Anomalous Zeeman Effect setup consists of:

  • Electromagnet & Variable Power Supply: Produces homogeneous fields up to 2.0 Tesla with bored pole pieces for longitudinal viewing.
  • Spectral Discharge Lamp: Electrodeless Sodium or Cadmium bulb placed in the pole gap.
  • Optical Collimator & Polarizer: Linear polarizer and quarter-wave plate to analyze linear and circular polarization states.
  • High-Resolution Dispersive Element: Fabry-Pérot interferometer etalon or diffraction grating spectrograph with resolving power $\lambda / \Delta\lambda > 10^5$.
  • Digital CCD Camera & Gaussmeter: Measures spatial fringe splitting and magnetic field strength.

🎓 Viva-Voce Examination Preparation

Q1: What is the fundamental difference between Normal and Anomalous Zeeman Effect?
The Normal Zeeman Effect occurs in singlet transitions where total spin S = 0 (e.g. Cadmium 643.8 nm). Because electron spin is absent, g_1 = g_2 = 1, and the spectral line splits into exactly three equidistant lines. The Anomalous Zeeman Effect occurs in atoms with non-zero spin (S ≠ 0, e.g. Sodium D-lines). Spin-orbit coupling produces distinct Landé g-factors for the upper and lower states (g_1 ≠ g_2), lifting the sub-level degeneracy and resulting in multiplets of 4, 6, 9, or more lines.
Q2: Why are π components absent when observing along the magnetic field (Longitudinal view)?
The π components correspond to transitions with ΔM_J = 0, where the electric dipole oscillates strictly parallel to the magnetic field vector B. Because electromagnetic waves are transverse, an oscillating electric dipole radiates zero energy along its axis of oscillation. Hence, along the longitudinal view (line of sight parallel to B), no π radiation reaches the detector.
Q3: How does this experiment allow the determination of the electron's specific charge (e/m)?
The wavenumber splitting Δν̃ is linearly proportional to the applied field: Δν̃ = [e / (4π m_e c)] · g_eff · B. By performing linear regression of splitting versus magnetic field, the slope m = Δν̃ / B directly yields (e/m) = 4π c · m / g_eff.
Q4: What are the selection rules for electric dipole Zeeman transitions?
The selection rules are ΔM_J = 0 (π components) and ΔM_J = ±1 (σ components). Transitions with J_upper = 0 to J_lower = 0 are forbidden. When J_upper = J_lower, M_upper = 0 to M_lower = 0 is forbidden for ΔM = 0.
